# Move flesh detail out of patch tokens

The best-supported way to get sharp, controllable dream flesh from a ~10M-parameter real-time net is a hybrid. Keep the triangle-token transformer for coarse work only: layout, lighting, and where flesh and organs go. Add a thin per-pixel deferred stage at full 240-line resolution. That stage reads surface-anchored inputs: rasterised UV, a texture-id embedding, normal and depth, a learned latent texture sampled per texture id, and dense organ-local coordinate and state channels. Eyes get special handling. Draw them from an explicit, gaze-rotated iris/lid representation, such as an atlas of the filmed frames indexed by gaze and blink, rather than inferring them from a token. Three findings back this and settle the questions the project was about to spend GPU time on. First, the averaged pattern is the mathematically expected answer when a pixel loss meets detail the inputs do not determine ([Blau & Michaeli](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2018/html/Blau_The_Perception-Distortion_Tradeoff_CVPR_2018_paper.html)). Second, even the 205M-parameter RenderFormer blurred textures through its 8x8 patch tokens, and its successor moved texture into a separate encoding with a dense decoder ([RenderFormer](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21925v1); [RenderFormer-V2](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.05738)). Third, every gaze-controllable neural head of 2023–2025 treats gaze as geometry, which roughly halves gaze error compared with conditioning on a gaze vector ([GazeGaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12981v2)). The full-resolution stage is affordable on an RX 580 if it stays a few layers of 16–64 channels, by my arithmetic about 1–2 GFLOP per frame. Adversarial training is still worth trying, but only after these input changes, and only on a small residual head whose gradient cannot reach the trunk. That firewalling is the main thing the earlier patch-GAN + Gram run lacked.

## The 16x16 token cannot carry the pattern, and more training will not fix it

The blur is a property of the loss, not a lack of training. Under a pixel-distance loss, the best output for any detail the inputs do not pin down is the average of all plausible targets. Blau and Michaeli prove that lowering distortion pushes outputs away from realism for every distortion measure, not just MSE ([Blau & Michaeli, CVPR 2018](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2018/html/Blau_The_Perception-Distortion_Tradeoff_CVPR_2018_paper.html)). A perfectly realistic sample has exactly **twice the MSE of the posterior mean** ([PMRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.00418v3)). With L1 + 0.1·LPIPS and targets whose fold phase, vein placement and painted-texture offset the net cannot read from its inputs, the "averaged flesh pattern" is the correct optimum. **This is well supported; no experiment is needed to confirm it.** There are two levers. Make the detail a function of the inputs, or give the generator a source of variation plus a loss that rewards one plausible sample over the mean.

The architecture makes the first lever hard to pull. **RenderFormer** uses **8x8-pixel** ray-bundle tokens, 768-d, and **205M parameters**, trained on 8 A100s for about 8 days. It supports only constant per-triangle reflectance, and its preliminary 32x32-texel-per-triangle textures came out **"blurred"** ([RenderFormer, arXiv 2505.21925](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21925v1)). RenderFormer-V2 (September 2026) did not fix this by scaling tokens. It encodes 32x32 texture patches with a VAE, uses windowed attention, and switches to a **DPT dense decoder** for output up to 2048². It trains with L1-on-log plus 0.05 LPIPS ([RenderFormer-V2 overview](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.05738)). I read V2 only at overview level, so its parameter counts and texture-fidelity numbers are unverified. Our net has 4x more pixels per token than RenderFormer, tokens a third as wide, and about 20x fewer parameters. By my arithmetic, each d=256 token must encode 256 pixels x 3 colours = 768 values before the head runs. A pixel-shuffle head then has to invent within-patch position from channels alone. That is the coordinate-transform problem CoordConv showed plain conv nets "fail spectacularly" at, and adding coordinate channels solves it with 10–100x fewer parameters ([CoordConv](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/1807.03247)). Recognition work confirms the trend. A 2025 patch-scaling study finds accuracy keeps improving as patches shrink, with **no saturation before 1x1** (ImageNet base: 80.5% at 16x16 to 84.6% at 1x1) ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03738)). Implicit decoders such as LIIF replace pixel-shuffle upsampling with an MLP that receives the query coordinate ([LIIF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.09161)). They still lean towards low frequencies unless given Fourier or texture-frequency features ([LTE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.08918)).

The literature does not contain a controlled comparison of patch-token and per-pixel G-buffer decoders on the same rendering task. Nor did I find a paper that combines a RenderFormer-style transformer with a DNR-style UV-sampled texture stage. The direction is consistent across RenderFormer's own failure, V2's fix, the patch-scaling study and CoordConv, so the hybrid is well grounded. Its exact form is still our design.

## A per-pixel deferred stage is cheap enough, if it stays thin

Deferred Neural Rendering is the template. It rasterises UV, bilinearly samples a learned multi-channel "neural texture", and decodes with a U-Net. The original used a **512x512x16 texture in a 4-level Laplacian hierarchy** and a **16M-parameter** U-Net. It rendered in **≈4 ms on a GTX 1080Ti** and trained with L1 for about 50k steps ([DNR, Thies et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.12356)). Three DNR results matter here. Its output was "much sharper" and "more temporally coherent" than pix2pix image translation, which the authors credit to the texture being anchored in 3D. Coarse proxies hurt little: going from 8K to 500 triangles only raised MSE from 11.07 to 18.4. And replacing every conv with 1x1 convs (a per-pixel MLP) gave **blurrier output**, which the authors blamed on geometry error that needs neighbourhood context to correct. PS1 geometry and UVs are exact, so this blur source should mostly disappear for us. A few 3x3 layers are still cheap insurance, and that part is an inference. DNR's other lesson is aliasing. A single texture overfits when magnified, so its MSE jumped to 8.46 at high resolution. The hierarchy fixed this, staying at 0.38 up to 2048² ([DNR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.12356)). PS1 corridors show the same texture across a huge range of distances, so use mip levels.

NVIDIA's 2023–2025 production line shows how small the decoder can be. Real-Time Neural Appearance Models use **8-channel mipmapped latent textures** decoded by MLPs of **2x16 to 3x64** hidden units (about 9–37 kB of weights). They train about 4–5 h per material on an RTX 4090. Two filtering lessons from that paper apply directly. Pick one of the two neighbouring mip levels at random rather than trilinearly blending latents, and train with exponentially distributed pixel footprints so every level is optimised. They also initialise latents from an encoder, then drop the encoder. Directly optimised latents keep their initialisation noise, and fine texels get far fewer gradient updates ([Zeltner et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.02678v2)). Neural Texture Compression uses a 2x64 decoder and also avoids trilinear latent filtering because it causes artefacts at boundaries ([NTC](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.17105)). Pixel Codec Avatars runs a shallow per-pixel MLP with a learned UV positional encoding in a fragment shader, at **90 FPS on Quest 2 against about 22 FPS for a conv baseline** ([PiCA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.04638)). For injecting G-buffer features into an image network, Intel's EPE showed that per-pixel scale-and-shift modulation from a G-buffer encoder ("rendering-aware denormalisation") beats SPADE in controlled tests ([EPE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.04619)).

The RX 580 caveat is real. Every shipped neural-shading feature assumes matrix units: NTC, neural materials, DirectX cooperative vectors, and FSR Redstone on RDNA 4 only ([AMD GPUOpen](https://gpuopen.com/learn/amd-fsr-redstone-developers-neural-rendering/); [DirectX cooperative vectors](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/directx/cooperative-vector/)). In Intel's demo, neural texture decoding went from **0.111 ms with cooperative vectors to 5.7 ms without** ([TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/105895/ai-texture-decompression-will-breathe-new-life-into-gpus-with-only-8gb-of-vram/index.html)). The RX 580 has **6.2 TFLOP/s at FP32 and the same at FP16 (1:1)**, with 256 GB/s of bandwidth ([TechPowerUp](https://techpowerup.com/gpudb/2938/radeon-rx-580)). So FP16 saves bandwidth and registers but not ALU time. My own arithmetic, not sourced: a 3x64 per-pixel MLP at 320x240 costs about 25 kFLOP per pixel, or about 1.9 GFLOP per frame. That is roughly 0.3 ms at peak and plausibly about 1 ms in scalar WGSL. A 64-channel FP32 full-resolution activation map is about 20 MB, so each read plus write costs about 0.15 ms of bandwidth. The practical rule is to keep full-resolution layers at 16–32 channels and fuse them, keeping tiles in workgroup memory as "fully fused" MLPs do ([NRC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.12372)). Leave the deep weights at 1/16 resolution, where the transformer already runs. No published WGSL or Polaris inference benchmarks exist, so the 1–2 ms estimate is the first thing to measure in the viewer. It needs no training run: random weights time the same as trained ones.

There is one more cheap move for static levels. NTC's "Inference on Load" decodes once into a texture cache and removes the per-frame neural cost ([Tom's Hardware](https://tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpus/benchmarking-nvidias-rtx-neural-texture-compression-tech-that-can-reduce-vram-usage-by-over-80-percent)). Flesh albedo and fold detail that does not depend on the view could be baked per surface when a level loads. The per-frame net would then handle only shading, eyes and pulsing. That is an inference, but it would also give temporal stability for free.

## Organs obey when they arrive as geometry, not as tokens

The pilot's 1–3% sensitivity to organ tokens is the textbook failure. Dense, pixel-aligned conditioning places content reliably. Sparse tokens or global vectors are weaker and need extra machinery. SPADE showed that layout fed through conv-norm layers gets "washed away" and must instead modulate every layer per pixel ([SPADE](https://nvlabs.github.io/SPADE)). Token-grounded diffusion (GLIGEN) trades control against quality ([ControlNet-XS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06573)), while a dense "spatial-semantic map" scores much higher on placement ([SSMG](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.10156v2)). **No head-to-head study of dense versus token conditioning in one small renderer exists.** The evidence is indirect but all points the same way.

Eyes are the strongest case. GazeNeRF, GazeGaussian and Roll Your Eyes all model the eyeball separately and rotate it rigidly by the gaze. GazeGaussian reaches **6.62° gaze error**. Its ablations without explicit eye rotation sit at about **13.5°**, and a head avatar with no eye stream reaches **30.96°** ([GazeGaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12981v2); [GazeNeRF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.04823)). Roll Your Eyes found exactly our failure: "the facial stream interferes with the eye region", and face Gaussians "deformed to compensate". Its fix is a **"blank loss"** that renders the face stream alone and forces it to a neutral fill inside the eye mask. Removing that loss raised gaze error from 4.41° to 5.40° ([Roll Your Eyes](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06136v2)). Blinks in head avatars come from geometry (FLAME eyelids) and need eye-specific fitting terms ([Neural Head Avatars](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.01554)). State-conditioned neural textures work too. Dynamic Neural Textures maps an expression code to a per-frame texture sampled on fixed geometry, in real time ([DNT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.06180)).

For neurofield this suggests an approach the papers did not test directly. The rasteriser writes organ channels per pixel: organ-local (u,v), radial distance, organ id and type, a coverage mask, and state channels. For eyes, the state is iris UV from the gaze-rotated eyeball plus a lid-opening mask from blink. For veins, it is sin/cos of the path coordinate minus the pulse phase. For sphincters, it is radius scaled by clench. The filmed eye footage is already indexed by gaze and blink, so the simplest eye source is an atlas of aligned eye crops over a (gaze, blink) grid. It is sampled through organ UV and interpolated, and fed in as dense RGB/feature channels or composited as RGB+alpha. A small MLP over (u, v, gaze, blink) is the smooth, learned alternative, at PiCA-class cost. The literature strongly supports explicit eye geometry, but **the atlas versus MLP choice and the vein/sphincter encodings are engineering inference; no published neural sphincter or vein renderer exists.**

If organ tokens stay in the transformer, four training levers have good support. The first is masked or biased cross-attention: patch queries outside an organ's screen box get −∞ to that token. Mask2Former's masked attention converged about **6x faster** ([Mask2Former](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.01527)). This is the attention bias already in the project's plan. The second is region loss weights of **20x** (Make-A-Scene's face parts) to **40x** (INSTA's mouth) ([Make-A-Scene](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.13131); [GaussianAvatars supp.](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/supplemental/Qian_GaussianAvatars_Photorealistic_Head_CVPR_2024_supplemental.pdf)). The third is per-pixel supervision that the organ exists. OASIS's segmentation discriminator gained about 6 FID and 5 mIoU ([OASIS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.04781)), and GazeGaussian's frozen gaze-estimator loss uses λ=0.1. The fourth is the blank loss. Two pitfalls are documented. Attention losses can reach near zero while the layout still fails ([MAC](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.10495v3)). And a newly injected dense condition is often ignored for thousands of steps, then "suddenly" obeyed; ControlNet's example flips at about step 6,133 ([ControlNet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.05543)). A pretrained or simpler condition encoder shortens that delay ([ControlNeXt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.06070); [CtrLoRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.09400)). So a 16k-step pilot could have stopped just before switch-on. Plotting organ sensitivity over training answers that for free from existing checkpoints.

## Sharpness: make detail deterministic first, then firewall any GAN

The cheapest and most stable fix for blur is the input-side one. Once fold phase, vein layout and eye frame are functions of the per-pixel inputs (UV, sculpt feature channels, organ state, time), plain L1 can learn them. The posterior mean then *is* the sharp target. This is a direct consequence of the perception-distortion result above, and it is why the sculpted-targets plan with explicit feature inputs is the right foundation. One condition is easy to miss. Any randomness in target generation, such as random fold seeds or per-frame offsets, must also be given to the net, or anchored to something it sees. Otherwise L1 averages it again.

For the remaining paired-loss tuning, practice is settled. Real-ESRGAN, the standard small sharp generator, uses **L1 1.0, VGG perceptual 1.0** with shallow layers down-weighted to 0.1, **Gram/style 0**, and **GAN 0.1** ([Real-ESRGAN config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xinntao/Real-ESRGAN/master/options/train_realesrgan_x4plus.yml)). Its perceptual weight is 10x ours. VGG-feature losses alone produce checkerboard and "painted canvas" artefacts. The artefact frequency halves with each max-pool deeper, ReLU-1_2 behaves best, and adding an adversarial term reduces the artefacts ([phase retrieval study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.05687); [Perceptual-optimisation asymmetry, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20878v1)). Gram matrices are an incomplete texture statistic. A crinkly fake texture can satisfy them, and sliced Wasserstein on VGG features is the documented, statistically complete replacement ([Heitz et al., CVPR 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021/html/Heitz_A_Sliced_Wasserstein_Loss_for_Neural_Texture_Synthesis_CVPR_2021_paper.html)). Focal frequency loss is a cheap sharpener for frequencies the inputs already determine ([FFL](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/html/Jiang_Focal_Frequency_Loss_for_Image_Reconstruction_and_Synthesis_ICCV_2021_paper.html)). It is still a paired loss and cannot produce stochastic detail. In short, perceptual and frequency losses sharpen edges the inputs fix, but they do not invent flesh.

The failed run maps cleanly onto known mistakes, and each has a published fix:

| Failure | Mechanism | Published fix |
|---|---|---|
| Trunk damaged, level textures blurred | GAN and Gram gradients flowed through the shared transformer | Train regression first, then fine-tune with GAN ([Real-ESRGAN](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.10833)). Put realism in a separate residual stage ([PMRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.00418v3); [Whang et al.](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/html/Whang_Deblurring_via_Stochastic_Refinement_CVPR_2022_paper.html)). Stop-gradient into the trunk is our inference. |
| Discriminator won | From-scratch patch D overfits on limited data | Small heads on frozen features: Projected GAN (40x faster to SOTA FID), vision-aided D, DINOv2 heads ([Projected GAN](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/9219adc5c42107c4911e249155320648-Abstract.html); [Vision-aided GAN](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Kumari_Ensembling_Off-the-Shelf_Models_for_GAN_Training_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf)) |
| Went NaN | Unregularised D, mixed precision | Zero-centred R1+R2 with relativistic loss ([R3GAN](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.05441)); adaptive GAN weight clamped at the head's last layer ([VQGAN-style](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.04632)); D loss in FP32 |
| 16-px grid | Patch-grid head plus deep-layer VGG/Gram gradients amplifying periodic structure | Resize-conv head (already a TODO); shallow perceptual layers; no Gram |

EPE is the closest prior art for G-buffer restyling of game frames. Its discriminator judges realism per semantic region by combining frozen VGG features with segmentation labels. It used small crops and patch matching to stop hallucinations, plus an R1-style penalty ([EPE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.04619)). The neurofield equivalent conditions D's heads on the rasteriser's material and organ ids, which is an inference. **No paper covers "GAN on one output of a shared multi-output net"**, so the firewalled residual head is our design built from established parts. It needs a small test.

Distilling diffusion into the runtime net is out of budget, since published one-step methods (ADD, DMD2, pix2pix-Turbo) train SD-sized students ([DMD2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14867); [pix2pix-Turbo](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12036v1)). Offline target refinement is possible. pix2pix-Turbo fine-tunes in about 7.5k steps and runs at 0.29 s per 512² image on an A6000, so a Modal L4 could refine a training set. But per-frame invention reintroduces stochastic targets and flicker unless seeds are tied to the surface. The sculpted-target pipeline already produces deterministic detail, so this is a late, optional step.

## Temporal stability comes from anchoring, then reprojection

No shipped game restyles frames with a neural net at runtime. Every vendor feature is upscaling, denoising, frame generation or texture/material decoding. The real-time generative renderers need datacentre or 4090-class hardware: AlayaRenderer-Flash at about 31 fps on an H200, DIAMOND at about 10 fps on a 3090 ([AlayaRenderer-Flash](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.18703); [DIAMOND](https://github.com/eloialonso/diamond)). The most useful precedent is Ioannou and Maddock's G-buffer-guided game stylisation. Their network fuses depth, normals and edges through a G-buffer encoder. A compute shader then lerps toward the motion-vector-reprojected previous output, weighted by depth. Removing that shader raised warping error from **0.1578 to 0.1803** and LPIPS from 0.3315 to 0.3652. Fine-tuning a general style model on their game data *worsened* temporal error ([Ioannou & Maddock, IEEE ToG](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/209827/1/G_buffer_Guided_NST_in_Games__IEEE_Transactions_on_Games_.pdf)). DNR's UV-anchored texture gave more coherent output than screen-space translation ([DNR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.12356)). FrameDiffuser feeds its previous output back as an input for long-horizon coherence ([FrameDiffuser](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16670v1)).

The ordering follows directly. Surface-anchored inputs (UV latents, sculpt features, organ-local coordinates, world-anchored noise) make detail 3D-consistent by construction. A reprojection blend with disocclusion rejection costs one fetch and a lerp per pixel. Feeding back the previous frame adds input channels and needs sequence training, so it is a last resort. A strong history blend smears hallucinated detail just as TAA does, so clamp it.

Before any new training, three free measurements decide most of this. The first is rank 1's timing. The second is the organ-sensitivity curve over the existing pilot checkpoints: if sensitivity was still rising, the net was heading for a ControlNet-style switch-on, and rank 4's weighting may be enough for the token path. The third is a per-material L1 and LPIPS split on held-out frames, plus a 2-D spectrum of outputs. That measures the averaging and the 1/16-px grid peak before and after, and catches any regression on level textures, the signature of trunk damage. During the rank 2–3 run, track organ-ablation sensitivity, which should rise far above 3%. If the eyes are rendered through the atlas, also track a frozen gaze/blink regressor's error on rendered eyes. Do not spend GPU time re-testing pixel-loss averaging, Gram's damage, or whether a gaze vector alone can steer an eye. The literature has answered all three.

## Conclusion

The project's two problems, blur and organ neglect, share one root cause. The net is asked to recover per-pixel facts from patch-level summaries: where the fold crest is, where the iris is, which frame of the blink is showing. The field's answer, from DNR to RenderFormer-V2 to gaze avatars, is to hand those facts to the pixels directly, through surface- and organ-anchored inputs, and to keep the transformer for things that really are global. Adversarial loss is then demoted from the main sharpening tool to an optional polish on a firewalled head. That reframing removes most of the risk the earlier GAN runs showed.

The biggest open question is engineering, not research: whether a thin full-resolution stage fits beside the transformer in about 10 ms of scalar WGSL on Polaris. No published benchmark covers it, it costs nothing to measure, and it should be measured first. If it fits, the sculpted-target data the project already generates can train the hybrid with plain L1 plus a stronger shallow perceptual loss. The question would shift from "how do we stop averaging" to "how much detail do we bake per level versus infer per frame".
